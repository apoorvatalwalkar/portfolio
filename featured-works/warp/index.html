Part One

Context

Warp is a startup modernizing the conventional terminal with artificial intelligence features. With automatic command completion, smart suggestions for tons of CLI tools, and numerous quality-of-life improvements, Warp aims to optimize every developer's workflow.

Through Brown's CSCI 1300 course on interaction design, we were tasked with designing a community page for Warp in an official partnership with Google Ventures. Warp Community is where users share everything from simple workflows to complex automations, whether they're an individual hobbyist or an industry professional who relies on Warp every day.

Getting Started

Present Factors

After being onboarded to the project, we spent time familiarizing ourselves with Warp, its AI-augmented terminal sessions, and the "meta" objects (Prompts, Workflows, Environment Variables, and Notebooks) used, saved, and retrieved in local use of Warp. We also surveyed the competitive landscape and synthesized a set of present factors to inform our design of Warp Community. (The full research writeup is available on request.)

Existing Considerations

Solutions today are scattered across informal channels, and existing tools don't offer much in the way of shared, structured community features.

Customer Motivations

Users want to move faster and more confidently with AI tools, and to learn from how others are already solving similar problems.

Problems with Existing Solutions

It's hard to find trustworthy examples, get feedback on your own work, or bring what others have discovered into your own projects.

The Project

What I Did

As part of the team, I participated in brainstorm sketching, low-fi, and high-fi prototyping. As a group, we each made 12 sketches independently, then combined them to generate a general layout we wanted to pursue. We each built part of the low-fi design in Balsamiq to send to the company for feedback.

After reading that feedback, we realized we'd lost sight of the problem at hand, so we readjusted and created a new design that was approved. From there, we built a high-fi prototype with connected flows in Figma, which we submitted as our final design and presented to executives at the startup, receiving really good feedback on our iterations.

A screen from the final high-fi Figma prototype

Key Takeaway

We needed to prioritize the success metrics. More than anything, we learned not to take pride in being right, but to admit when we're wrong so we can improve.

Insights

Iterative Feedback

Our initial low-fi mockup missed the success metrics and key user we'd been given. Practicing iterative design out of necessity, we saw firsthand how the process leads to a product that better serves the user, and how much time we could have saved by asking the right questions earlier.

Designing for the User

It's easy to lose sight of a project's defined success metrics. Refocusing on how Warp Community related to Warp Terminal, rather than just platform-building, let us arrive at a design the Warp team was genuinely excited about.

What Shipped

Our final design was praised for how it handled object creation and the publication flow into the local Warp Drive, and for considering how much developers care about their IDE and terminal setup.
